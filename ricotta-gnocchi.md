# Ricotta Gnocchi with Brown Butter, Pepitas & Basil

*Pillowy 30-minute gnocchi — no potatoes to bake or rice — in brown butter with toasted pepitas and basil*

Scaled to feed five with real leftovers.

## Ingredients

- 2 lb whole-milk ricotta, well drained
- 2 large eggs, plus 1 extra yolk
- 6 oz Parmesan or pecorino, finely grated, plus more to serve
- 2 cups all-purpose flour, plus more for dusting
- 2 tsp kosher salt, plus more for the water
- ¼ tsp freshly grated nutmeg
- 10 tbsp unsalted butter
- ½ cup pepitas (Original Porcini are great here)
- 1 lemon, zested, half of it juiced
- 1 cup fresh basil leaves, torn
- ½ tsp black pepper

## Steps

1. **Drain the ricotta (do this the night before).** Line a sieve with cheesecloth, add the ricotta, set over a bowl, and drain in the fridge overnight (or at least 4 hours). Discard the whey. This single step decides whether your gnocchi are light or leaden. *(timer: 4 hr)*
2. **Make the dough.** In a large bowl, mix the drained ricotta with the eggs and yolk, the grated cheese, kosher salt, and nutmeg until smooth. Add the flour in two additions, folding with a spatula just until a soft, slightly tacky dough comes together. Stop the moment it holds — every extra stir adds chew.
3. **Test one.** Pinch off a walnut of dough, roll it, and boil it in salted water. If it holds together and tastes tender, you're done. If it falls apart, fold in 2 more tablespoons of flour and test again. This 3-minute check saves the whole batch. *(timer: 3 min)*
4. **Roll and cut.** On a well-floured counter, divide the dough into 6 pieces. Roll each into a 3/4-inch rope with light hands, then cut into 1-inch pillows. Set them on floured sheet pans, not touching. Roll each over the back of a fork for ridges if you want them — optional, but they hold more butter. *(timer: 25 min)*
5. **Boil in batches.** Bring a large pot of well-salted water to a gentle boil — a hard rolling boil tears them apart. Cook in batches: drop them in, and when they float, give them 30 more seconds, then lift out with a spider. About 2–3 minutes each batch. *(timer: 3 min)*
6. **Toast the pepitas and brown the butter.** While the water heats, toast the pepitas in a dry skillet over medium until they pop and smell nutty, 3–4 minutes. Set aside. In the same pan, melt the butter over medium and cook, swirling, until the foam subsides and the milk solids turn deep amber and smell like toasted nuts, 4–6 minutes. Kill the heat immediately — it goes from brown to burnt fast. *(timer: 8 min)*
7. **Sauce it.** Add the lemon juice and zest from the lemon to the brown butter — it will sputter. Add the drained gnocchi with a splash of pasta water and toss gently over low heat until glossy, about a minute. Fold in the pepitas, basil, and black pepper. *(timer: 2 min)*
8. **Serve.** Plate and finish with plenty of grated cheese and a few more torn basil leaves.

## Notes

DRAINING IS THE WHOLE GAME. Wet ricotta means more flour means dense gnocchi. Set it in a cheesecloth- or coffee-filter-lined sieve over a bowl in the fridge overnight; you may pull off half a cup of whey. Skip this and the recipe fights you.

Leftovers: uncooked gnocchi freeze beautifully — freeze them solid in a single layer on the sheet pan, then bag them, and boil straight from frozen (add a minute). Cooked leftovers are best pan-fried in butter the next day until crisp on two sides, which some people prefer to the original. Sauce them only as much as you're eating; store the rest naked.

Potato version, if you'd rather: bake 3 lb russets at 400°F until soft, rice them hot, cool slightly, then mix with 2 eggs, 2 tsp salt, and about 2 cups flour. More work, chewier and more substantial result, and much less forgiving of overmixing.

Sauce alternatives from the current list: Seven Sisters nettle-hazelnut pesto loosened with pasta water, or a quick fresh tomato sauce from heirlooms and basil. Sage is not on the market list this week, which is why this version leans on pepitas and lemon instead.

## Skim ricotta or no time to drain

These adjustments came up after the card was written, for when only skim-milk ricotta is available and there's no time for the overnight drain.

**Fast-press the ricotta (30–40 minutes).** Spread it in a thin layer on a clean kitchen towel or four layers of paper towel, lay another towel on top, and press firmly with your palms. Swap in dry towels once and repeat. Surface area does what time would have — a thin layer pressed twice pulls off nearly as much whey as an overnight sit. Alternatively, gather it in cheesecloth and hang it from a cabinet knob over a bowl with a can resting on top; 45 minutes gets you most of the way. Don't be gentle — it should come out noticeably drier and more crumbly than it went in.

**Add the fat back.** Use two extra yolks instead of one, bump the Parmesan to 7 oz, and stir a couple tablespoons of soft butter into the cheese before the flour goes in. A few ounces of cream cheese or mascarpone folded in works even better if you can get it.

**Handle gently.** Use a lighter touch rolling, a well-floured board, and keep the water at a bare simmer rather than a boil. Slightly wetter dough is more fragile going in, but it firms up as soon as it cooks.

**The test-gnocco is non-negotiable.** Pinch off a walnut-sized piece, roll it into a rough ball, and drop it into a small pot of salted simmering water. Let it cook until it floats plus another thirty seconds, then fish it out and eat it. If it held its shape and tastes tender, roll the rest. If it fell apart or dissolved into cloudy shreds, fold in 2 more tablespoons of flour and test again — better to land at 2½ cups of flour and have them hold together than to be scraping shreds out of the pot. If it held but feels dense, rubbery, or bready, there's too much flour or the dough got overworked; it's still perfectly edible, but handle the next batch more gently. Ricotta moisture varies a lot between brands and batches, so the flour amount is a target you sneak up on, not a measurement.
