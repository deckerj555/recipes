# Modern Sukiyaki

*Restaurant-style sukiyaki at the table: a scratch dashi-based warishita, a Kansai-style first round of beef seared in its own fat, then a Kanto-style simmer, with udon at the end. About 1 hour, most of it prep. The warishita can be made days ahead.*

## Ingredients

**Warishita (sauce)**

- ½ cup sake
- ½ cup mirin (real mirin, not corn-syrup "aji-mirin")
- ½ cup soy sauce
- 3 tbsp sugar
- 1½ cups dashi (kombu and katsuobushi), plus more for thinning at the table

**Beef**

- 2 lb well-marbled ribeye or chuck eye, sliced 1/16 inch thick across the grain (or pre-sliced "sukiyaki" or "shabu" beef from an Asian market)
- 1 piece of beef suet, about 1 inch (ask the butcher, or trim it from the ribeye). You can use 1 tbsp neutral oil instead.
- 1 tbsp sugar, for the first round

**The pot**

- 2 negi, or 1 large leek, cut on a sharp diagonal into 2-inch pieces
- ¼ head napa cabbage, cut into 2-inch pieces, stems and leaves kept separate
- 8 fresh shiitake, stems off, caps scored with an X
- 1 package (7 oz) enoki, root end trimmed, pulled into small clumps
- 1 bunch shungiku (chrysanthemum greens). Mizuna or watercress also work.
- 1 block (14 oz) firm tofu, pressed 15 minutes and cut into 8 slabs
- 1 package (7 oz) shirataki noodles

**To serve**

- 4–6 pasteurized eggs, one per person in a small bowl for dipping
- Steamed short-grain rice
- 2–4 portions frozen sanuki udon, for the finish

## Steps

1. **Make the warishita.** Bring the sake and mirin to a boil in a small saucepan and boil for 1 minute to cook off the alcohol. Add the soy and sugar and stir until the sugar dissolves. Off the heat, stir in the dashi. Taste: it should be noticeably sweeter and saltier than a soup, because it will be diluted at the table. Keeps a week in the fridge. *(timer: 5 min)*
2. **Slice the beef.** If slicing it yourself, freeze the beef until firm but not hard, then slice as thin as you can across the grain. Lay the slices out on a platter, overlapping, and refrigerate. *(timer: 45 min)*
3. **Sear the tofu.** Pat the slabs dry and sear them in a dry nonstick or well-seasoned cast-iron pan over high heat until both broad sides are browned. This stands in for yaki-dofu: the crust keeps the tofu from crumbling and soaks up the sauce. *(timer: 6 min)*
4. **Blanch the shirataki.** Drain, rinse, and boil the shirataki for 2 minutes to get rid of the alkaline smell, then drain and cut into manageable lengths. *(timer: 2 min)*
5. **Set up the table.** Arrange the vegetables, tofu, and shirataki on a platter. Put a heavy shallow pan (a cast-iron sukiyaki pan, a 12-inch skillet, or an electric skillet) on a tabletop burner. Each person gets an egg beaten in a small bowl, plus a bowl of rice.
6. **First round, Kansai style.** Heat the pan over medium-high and rub the suet around until the bottom is glossy with fat. Lay in a few slices of beef, sprinkle with a pinch of sugar, and add a splash of warishita. Flip once. When the beef is just pink, it goes straight into the egg and gets eaten right away. This round is the best bite of the night. *(timer: 1 min)*
7. **Build the pot.** Sear the negi in the fat for a minute so it picks up some color. Add the napa stems, shiitake, tofu, and shirataki in separate groups. Keep the shirataki away from where the beef will go, since its calcium hydroxide toughens meat. Pour in warishita to about ½ inch deep. *(timer: 2 min)*
8. **Simmer and cook in rounds.** Simmer gently until the vegetables are tender. Then add the napa leaves, enoki, and beef a few slices at a time, and add the shungiku last because it only needs 30 seconds. Everyone pulls food out as it's done and dips it in egg. *(timer: 8 min)*
9. **Manage the sauce.** As the sauce reduces it gets saltier and sweeter. Add dashi (or water) a splash at a time to keep it balanced, and top up with warishita when the pan runs low. Refill with ingredients as people eat.
10. **Finish with udon.** When the main ingredients are gone, thin the remaining sauce with dashi to a soup-like strength. Add the udon and simmer until hot and glossy. Pour any leftover egg over the noodles and cover until it just sets. *(timer: 3 min)*

## Notes

**What's modern here, compared with [Dad's sukiyaki](sukiyaki.md):** dashi-based warishita with sake and mirin in place of soy, sugar and beef broth; a first round of beef seared in its own fat; marbled ribeye or chuck eye in place of lean tenderloin; seared tofu; shirataki in place of bean thread; negi, shiitake, enoki and shungiku in place of onion, button mushrooms and spinach; a raw-egg dip; and udon at the end to use up the sauce.

**Kansai vs. Kanto.** Kansai cooks sear the beef in fat with sugar and soy and add liquid gradually. Kanto cooks simmer everything in premixed warishita. This recipe does a Kansai first round, then switches to Kanto for the rest, which is how many sukiyaki restaurants now serve it.

**Eggs.** Raw egg is traditional and cools each bite while coating it. Use pasteurized eggs (Davidson's, for example). Anyone who doesn't want it can skip the egg, or dip in a soft onsen tamago (63°C for 45 minutes) instead.

**Beef.** Marbling matters more than tenderness. Chuck eye is a good, less expensive substitute for ribeye. Slices any thicker than about 1/16 inch overcook before they absorb the sauce.

**Mild.** There's no heat in the pot. Shichimi can go on the table for anyone who wants it.

**Leftovers.** Simmer leftover beef, onion, and sauce briefly, pour beaten egg over it, and serve over rice for a sukiyaki don the next day.

## Sukiyaki don (weeknight version)

For a 30-minute weeknight bowl: sliced onion and ½ lb shaved beef per two people, simmered in ½ cup warishita thinned with ¼ cup dashi. Add beaten egg and cover just until it sets, then serve over rice with scallions. It uses the leftover warishita.
